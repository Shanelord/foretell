U.S. Impact & Growth Clock

– Foretell Foundation

Issue. AI and robots may stimulate the U.S. economy but also affect the ability of people to earn within certain sectors. The rate at which this occurs will differ between sectors and even between areas and ages. Currently, it is unknown where these changes will occur. Society therefore needs to detect and respond effectively to change using actual data, not future predictions.

Method. Foretell Foundation, a non-profit think tank, developed the U.S. Impact & Growth Clock. This interactive public resource illustrates the likely level of production by machines, the likely level of job replacement, the potential for public backing, and the economy’s preparedness. Data is marked as either actual or modelled.

Outcomes to date. This interactive resource enables comparison between how quickly machine production could grow and the extent to which society is prepared to share out the benefits. However, its predictions remain untested. The next step is a monitoring process, built with partners, that will collate real-world information together with examples of where even our own advice has proved inaccurate.

When should we respond?

Students may wish to take out loans to study for an arts-related subject. Colleges can specify both the cost and the nature of study, but have no information on whether the entry-level jobs for graduates will still be available in four years’ time. There may initially be no advertisement for entry-level artistic positions if there is reduced demand for them. By the time data on graduate salaries and loan difficulties demonstrates change, a new cohort of students may already have begun studying.

This is why Foretell has constructed the clock. Waiting for society to experience difficulty means people suffer negative consequences for which they had limited preparation. Premature intervention risks closing potentially valuable career routes and/or investing public funds in benefits that have not yet materialised. Differing sectors could also require responses at different times.

There is no consensus on the speed of change

Whether or not super-intelligence is in our future, there is currently debate about the speed, and indeed the location, of change in the workplace. Disagreement exists between those who create and those who regulate the technology.

  • Demis Hassabis, Chief Executive of Google DeepMind, has said AI will have ten times the impact of the Industrial Revolution, arriving ten times as quickly (Fast Company).
  • Sam Altman, Chief Executive of OpenAI, calls the next few years “a painful adjustment.” He explains that society is moving from managing scarcity to managing abundance, and that this would transform how capitalism has worked (Fortune).
  • Dario Amodei, Chief Executive of Anthropic, has described one scenario in which wealth becomes so accessible that society reaches a “universal high income” (Business Insider, via Yahoo).
  • Kristalina Georgieva, Managing Director of the IMF, calls the effect of AI on jobs a “slow-moving tsunami” affecting around 40% of jobs worldwide. She says young people entering the labour market are the first to worry about (Fortune).

Opinions also evolve. In May 2026, Sam Altman said that entry-level white-collar job losses had been smaller than he expected (Fortune). Jensen Huang of Nvidia has argued that AI creates as many jobs as it displaces, and that recent job losses were not driven by AI (AFP, via BNN Bloomberg).

As knowledgeable people continue to update their views, no single prediction should inform our response. Rather, local and current data should underpin decisions. This is the basis for the clock.

Evidence to date

Two studies highlight the uneven nature of change.

In a study of 5,172 customer-service agents at one company, access to an AI assistant raised the number of issues resolved per hour by an average of 15%. Most of the benefit went to less experienced staff (Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, Quarterly Journal of Economics).

The Stanford Digital Economy Lab analysed ADP payroll data to June 2026. It found no widespread, economy-wide job displacement. However, employment among 22- to 25-year-olds in AI-exposed occupations was 19% below where it would have been had it kept pace with less-exposed occupations. No such gap was seen among experienced workers (Stanford Digital Economy Lab). As the researchers state, these are descriptive findings, not causal inferences.

The same technology produced two different signals: a tool that helped people already in a job, and a narrowing path for people trying to enter one.

What the clock displays currently

Three areas are displayed side by side: how much machines may produce, how much human work may be displaced, and how many individuals could be supported under a specified support policy.

The likely extent of job replacement is shown for three, seven, ten and fifteen years’ time. A crossover point between human and machine GDP is also shown in model form, and state data is available. Economic support, capacity to absorb job losses, education and infrastructure are rated for preparedness. Showing these factors makes the underlying assumptions visible. The crossover point, the extent of job replacement and the potential support stipend are all modelled. As yet, the clock has not assessed how reliable these models are, nor whether those following its advice perform better.

Varied evidence, varied action

Ideally, the clock’s advice will adapt to the pattern observed locally.

  • Workers produce more while pay and hours hold up. Assess whether the gains reach workers as better pay, shorter hours or increased safety.
  • Experienced staff stay employed, but entry-level and graduate opportunities fall. Publish graduate employment, pay and indebtedness figures before students begin study. Pilot remunerated apprenticeships and adapt the affected parts of a course.
  • Jobs disappear in one sector while similar jobs open in a neighbouring one. Provide financial support and re-training linked to those roles. Build training around the skills required, then monitor employment, retention and salary outcomes.
  • Productivity rises but labour and household incomes fall across several sectors. Evaluate shorter working hours, financial support, direct payments and co-ownership. Illustrate the cost, the funding and the number of households helped under each.

Mechanism for determining response points

Foretell is not suggesting what the response points should be. It is proposing that an appropriate mechanism must be in place to determine them.

For precisely defined processes, such as initial artistic design or claims processing, the mechanism would assess how much of the work formerly performed by humans machines now undertake. Throughout, it would track labour market change, including employment, pay, hours, and graduate and entry-level job trends. Analysis of this information would then show where response points belong. These may differ between sectors and areas, and instances of over- and under-responding could also be recognised.

As changes occur at differing paces and to differing extents, a single national response point would be misleading. People experiencing earlier income reduction would already be eligible for support.

Assessing preparedness

For each sector and area, the clock would establish who can respond to change and what indicators show that the response is effective.

  • Private industry: Which tasks have changed? Who is still being hired? How have the pay and hours of re-employed workers evolved?
  • Education: How much are students borrowing? What jobs do graduates secure? Which skills still need to be taught to enable oversight and recovery?
  • Government: How swiftly is support granted to affected households? What proportion of publicly funded trainees obtain and keep well-paid work, in roles similar to their previous ones?
  • Infrastructure: Can energy, IT and communication systems sustain adoption? How rapidly can trained personnel restore critical systems if they fail?

Each data point will include a source, a date and a designation of actual or modelled status. Advice will be updated as new data arrives, including when hiring recovers or when an early warning proves to have been excessive.

Ownership and beneficiaries

Private-sector machine ownership can raise productivity. Higher profits may accrue to the owner, a technology provider may charge for services, employees may benefit, or cost savings may be passed on to customers. Each of these routes affects household and federal government finances in distinct ways.

RAND’s 2026 analysis indicates that, without a major increase in corporate taxation, the extra corporate profits generated by AI would not fully compensate for the loss of labour-related tax revenue. The severity depends on whether displaced workers find new work and on how AI is priced (RAND). Yale’s Budget Lab, modelling an AI-driven economic expansion, finds that total federal revenue usually rises in its scenarios, depending on the size of the expansion and how its gains are divided between capital and labour (Yale Budget Lab). Divergent assumptions underpin these contrasting conclusions, which is why the clock shows its assumptions.

It would also display how a proposed citizen stipend would be financed, contrasting lost labour-related tax revenue with additional revenue raised. Spending on support, and any resulting government borrowing and interest, would be included. From a household perspective, the clock would contrast post-tax income with expenditure on necessities and debt repayments. Even with a significant price reduction, households can become worse off if their income is falling more rapidly.

During the clock’s development, one option raised was that each child could be given a productive asset at birth, for example a robot that works to generate income for fundamental needs. Later in life, individuals could be free to invest in further assets. Alternatively, all children could share a collective productive asset fund. The clock could contrast these two options with a tax-financed stipend, considering acquisition and upkeep costs, investment risk, and the support a household would receive in a poor year. Children from households that own no machines are key to analysing how far each policy reaches.

Indications of success

The first practical test is to follow one sector within one area through a real-world response. Has there been a sustained change in employers’ entry-level recruitment? Have prospective students been made aware of it before incurring debt? Has an amended course or remunerated apprenticeship resulted in lasting employment? Evaluating this would allow Foretell to assess the quality of its advice. As with successful responses, an equally rigorous analysis of unsuccessful ones would be undertaken. This may involve early warnings that were excessive, response points set in the wrong place, or training programmes that failed to result in lasting employment.

Over a longer timeframe, considerations will extend beyond labour. A four-day week at constant pay can appear, in terms of hours, identical to compulsory four-day working, but for an individual these are very different experiences. The clock will consider whether people can afford necessities, manage their time flexibly, develop careers and/or study, engage in artistic pursuits and participate in community activities. Such matters are the underlying reason for examining machine expansion.
